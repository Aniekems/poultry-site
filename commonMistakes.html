Common Broiler Farming Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Starting a broiler farm may look simple on paper: buy day-old chicks, provide feed and water, manage them properly, and sell them when they reach market weight. In reality, small management mistakes can affect growth, increase costs, and reduce profitability. In more serious cases, poor management can contribute to disease, high mortality, or significant financial losses. Whether you're raising broilers for the first time or have managed several batches, here are some common management mistakes to watch out for — and how to avoid them.

Poor Brooding Temperature Management

The first two weeks of a broiler’s life are extremely important, and getting the brooding temperature right is one of the biggest challenges during the early stages of broiler production. Too much heat and chicks spread out, pant, and drink excessively instead of eating. Too little heat and they huddle together, get chilled, and become vulnerable to disease. Some farmers get the brooder heat working and then leave it unchanged, without adjusting as the chicks grow or as outside weather changes overnight.

The chicks themselves will tell you if the temperature is right — watch how they distribute themselves under the heat source. Chicks that are spread out evenly and moving about freely are generally comfortable. Chicks piled together near the heat source are too cold, while chicks scattered far to the edges of the pen, panting, are too hot. See our Brooding page for more on brooding.

Underestimating Feed Quantity and Quality

A common mistake is underfeeding birds to save money, not realizing that this can slow growth and increase the number of days needed to reach market weight — which may actually cost more in the long run. On the other side, over-filling feeders wastes just as much money: birds scratch out excess feed onto the litter, where it gets soiled and can spoil in humid conditions, sometimes leading to mold contamination if not cleared promptly.

Just as important as quantity is feed quality. Some farmers choose cheaper feeds to cut costs without considering whether they provide adequate levels of essential nutrients. Poorly formulated feed can result in inadequate protein, energy, vitamins, or minerals, leading to slower growth, poorer feed conversion, and, in some cases, nutritional deficiencies that contribute to leg problems. If you're unsure how much feed your birds need to reach a certain weight, our Feed Planning page will walk you through it.

Delaying or Skipping Vaccination

This is one of the costliest mistakes, because the damage often doesn't show up until it's too late to fix. Some farmers delay vaccination because chicks "look healthy," or they skip a dose because of cost, not realizing that a single disease outbreak (like Newcastle disease or Gumboro) can cause severe illness, high mortality, or even wipe out an entire flock, resulting in major financial losses. Others vaccinate on the wrong schedule, or administer vaccines incorrectly, reducing effectiveness.

A consistent vaccination programme based on the recommended schedule for the vaccines being used is an important part of broiler management. Check our Vaccination page for the vaccination schedule and correct dosing and administration steps.

Poor Housing and Ventilation

Farmers sometimes focus so much on feeding and medication that they overlook housing conditions. Overcrowding is extremely common — cramming too many birds into a small space increases heat stress, ammonia buildup from droppings, and disease transmission. Poor ventilation makes these problems worse, especially during hot weather, when birds can suffer severe heat stress and, in extreme conditions, die even when other aspects of management are good.

Litter management is another overlooked area. Wet, dirty litter creates favorable conditions for harmful microorganisms and increases ammonia buildup, which can damage the birds' respiratory systems. Housing isn't just about keeping birds enclosed — it directly affects their comfort, health, growth, and survival.

Inconsistent Water Supply

Water is sometimes treated as an afterthought, but broilers consume substantially more water than feed by weight, and their water requirements can increase considerably during hot weather. Some farmers let waterers run dry, especially overnight, or fail to clean them regularly, allowing dirt, algae, and microorganisms to build up. Insufficient or contaminated water can reduce feed intake, slow growth, cause dehydration, and increase the risk of health problems.

Clean, constantly available water should be treated with the same seriousness as feed and medication.

No Record Keeping

Some farmers run their farm largely from memory — how many chicks were bought, what was spent on feed, which day vaccines were given, how many birds died and any known causes of death. Without records, it's almost impossible to know whether your farm is actually profitable, or to catch patterns like recurring losses in the same week of each cycle. Even a simple notebook tracking daily feed given, mortality, and costs can reveal problems early enough to fix them.

Ignoring Biosecurity

Farmers sometimes underestimate how easily disease can enter and spread around a poultry farm — through contaminated equipment, dirty drinking water, wet or dirty litter, rodents, wild birds, other animals, visitors, or even on the farmer's own boots after visiting another poultry farm. Dirty drinkers, feeders, and other equipment allow harmful microorganisms to spread among birds just as easily.

Simple measures can significantly reduce disease risk. Keep drinkers, feeders, and all equipment clean. Maintain dry, clean litter throughout the growing cycle — wet and dirty litter (especially in the deep litter system where the birds are in direct contact with the litter material) can significantly increase the risk of disease and other health problems. Use a footbath filled with disinfectant solution at the entrance so anyone entering passes through it. Protect feed from contamination and moisture. Remove dead birds promptly and dispose of them properly, away from the farm, to prevent disease from lingering in your environment. Limit access to your farm from other farms and minimize contact between your broilers and other animals, since they can carry pathogens even if they appear healthy.

Good biosecurity doesn't require expensive equipment — it's about taking simple, consistent steps to reduce the risk of disease entering your farm and spreading through your flock.

Final Thoughts

Most of these mistakes aren't caused by lack of effort — they're caused by lack of information at the start. The good news is that every one of them is preventable with consistent, informed management. If you're new to broiler farming, take time to go through our Brooding, Feeding, Vaccination, and Housing guides before your next batch of chicks arrives. A little preparation upfront saves far more than it costs.
